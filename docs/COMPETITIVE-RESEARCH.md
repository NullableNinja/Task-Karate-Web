# Martial arts website comparison

This is a ten-site audit used to identify what the Task Karate public site is still missing. It includes nine external martial arts schools and the current Task Karate site as a local-business control. The goal is to borrow useful information architecture and trust patterns, not copy another school’s visual identity.

## Sites reviewed

1. [All Star Jiu Jitsu](https://www.allstarjiujitsu.com/) — community-first positioning, real student photography, programs, testimonials, FAQ, and a repeated free-class path.
2. [Mission MMA & Fitness](https://missionmmachicago.com/) — immediate location, disciplines, reviews, coaches, schedule, FAQ, and free-trial path.
3. [Renzo Gracie Academy](https://renzogracieacademy.com/) — established year, location, clear discipline list, instructor directory, prices, first-visit information, etiquette, and FAQ.
4. [Gentle Art Dojo](https://gentleartdojoflorida.com/) — location/program routing is the first decision, which is effective for a multi-location school.
5. [Blue Wave Brazilian Jiu Jitsu](https://bluewavejiujitsu.com/) — age-specific programs, schedule/pricing CTA, reviews, contact capture, and a clear beginner/family welcome.
6. [The Dojo NYC](https://www.thedojonyc.com/) — restrained photography, simple programs, lineage/community proof, and a repeated free-trial action.
7. [Anderson’s Martial Arts Academy](https://www.andersonsmartialarts.com/) — exact location/contact, established year, instructor lineage, reviews, program breadth, schedule, and a strong beginner statement above the fold.
8. [American Combat Gym](https://americancombatgym.com/) — clear “no experience needed” message, program cards with level/age/duration, coach credentials, reviews, and schedule links.
9. [Neutral Ground Academy](https://www.ngmke.com/) — especially relevant regional example: beginner-friendly language, safety-first culture, program-specific pages, rules/etiquette, reviews, and a visible free trial.
10. [Task Karate current site](https://taskkarateschool.com/) — control reference for current business information and the published schedule.

## What the strongest sites consistently do

### 1. They sell the first visit, not an abstract identity

The best hero sections name the city, audience, program, and next action. “Beginners welcome,” “no experience needed,” “ages 5–14,” “schedule and pricing,” and “book a free trial” appear immediately. The current Task redesign says something pleasant but abstract: “Martial arts for real life.” It needs a more concrete first-visit promise beside that line.

### 2. They make belonging visible

Real students, family photos, named testimonials, and calm explanations do more work than stylized fight imagery. Task has authentic photos, but the current hero image is visually busy and the site has no real student quotes, review proof, or “what your first class is like” section.

### 3. They explain the school through people

Strong sites show instructor credentials, lineage, coaching roles, and sometimes an established year near the top. Task has Randy’s biography, but it is buried on the About route and the homepage does not establish who is teaching or why a new student should trust the instruction.

### 4. They answer beginner questions before asking for contact information

The strongest examples include a first-visit guide or FAQ: what to wear, whether gear is needed, whether beginners train separately, how long class lasts, and what happens when someone arrives. Task currently asks visitors to call or email without reducing enough uncertainty first.

### 5. They turn programs into decisions

Programs are not only descriptive cards. They state age, experience level, goal, class format, and the next relevant schedule action. Task’s program copy is friendly but still makes the visitor sort out too much, especially for Kids, Teens/Adults, Eskrima, weapons, and sparring.

### 6. They use schedule information as a conversion surface

The first schedule attempt was still too much information at once: 57 pills in a six-column wall. The stronger pattern is: choose audience/program first, then see the exact times and eligibility. The implementation now uses separate program schedules, rank rows, compact time/duration pills, and program/belt/day filters.

### 7. They show proof without becoming corporate

Reviews, student quotes, instructor details, and a real address create confidence. Task currently has no testimonials or review surface, so the site asks the visitor to trust the design and copy alone.

## What Task Karate should not copy

- Fake urgency, popups, or aggressive lead funnels.
- Generic champion/fighter language that does not sound like Task Karate.
- Invented ratings, testimonials, prices, credentials, or instructor claims.
- Rules rewritten by the website author.
- Portal/check-in features on the public site.
- A dense multi-location navigation model that does not apply to Task.

## Recommended next design direction

The next iteration should be a calmer, information-first dojo site:

1. A compact hero with the actual welcome offer, location, audience, and one primary action.
2. A “Which class is for me?” decision block before the full program directory.
3. An instructor/trust block on the homepage, not only About.
4. A real first-visit guide and FAQ using only approved/current information.
5. A schedule that filters by student body/program first, then belt group and day.
6. A restrained proof section using real quotes only after the school supplies or approves them.
7. More varied image composition: one calm space/portrait image, one class image, and one community image instead of making every page lead with giant type over a busy group photo.

The exact Task Karate rules remain protected copy:

- Don’t use it the wrong way!
- Be respectful!
- Do your best!
